Volume I — Culture

A visual language

Across African societies, hairstyles and dress signalled age, status, marital state, lineage and belief. This page centres documented historical evidence — photographs, engravings and rock art from African and African-descended contexts.

From the archives

Real hairstyles from African sources

Authentic period records — photographs and engravings made from the 1880s to the 1930s — of hair worn as identity, status and art.

Sources: Roy Sieber & Frank Herreman (eds.), Hair in African Art and Culture (Museum for African Art/Prestel, 2000); A. Transatlantic slavery era (17th–19th century)

Braids, styles and headwraps

Documented hairstyles and headwraps of enslaved African and African-descended women, drawn from period photographs and engravings. Direct evidence from earlier centuries is limited; where possible, captions reflect what is visible and verifiable.

Sources: Period photographs/prints (New Orleans 1850s; emancipated people brought from Louisiana, December 1863, Myron H. Kimball; Marguerite, a former slave, 1880). See Metropolitan Museum of Art and Library of Congress collections; descriptions limited to verifiably visible features.

Cloth, music & memory

Culture as living knowledge

Textiles

Cloth as language

Kente motifs carry proverbs; adire and bogolan are dyed and painted with meaning; patterns encode history, status and belief — cloth you can read.

Orature

The griot's memory

Mande jeliw (griots) carried genealogies, law and history in song, accompanying themselves on the kora. Oral tradition was a rigorous archive, kept accurate across generations.

Music

Rhythm as structure

Polyrhythm, call-and-response and improvisation within a shared frame shaped music across the continent — and, through the diaspora, much of the modern world's music.

Rites & philosophy

Masks, rites & Ubuntu

Gelede masquerades honour mothers and community; Ifa divination frames fate and ethics; Ubuntu — “I am because we are” — puts relationship at the centre of personhood.
