The Middle Passage

The forced crossing of the Atlantic — the middle leg of a three-way trade. Roughly 12.5 million Africans were embarked on slave ships; millions did not survive the sea. This is how the crossing worked, and how we know what we know about it.

A note before you read

This page describes the lethal, forced Atlantic crossing of enslaved African people. It includes archival ship plans and photographs of forts and holding compounds, shown to expose the system's inhumanity. Names and details of the dead rarely survive; those of the enslaved often survive only in ship records. It is written to honour them.

What the Middle Passage was

The transatlantic slave trade ran as a rough triangle. European ships carried manufactured goods — cloth, guns, brass, rum — to African coasts. There they were exchanged for enslaved people, who were carried across the Atlantic to the Americas. Finally, colonial produce — sugar, tobacco, cotton, rum — was shipped back to Europe. The middle leg, the ocean crossing with its human cargo, gave the route its name: the Middle Passage.

The trade was not incidental to the Atlantic economy — it was central to it. The labour of millions of enslaved Africans built the plantation economies of the Caribbean and the Americas, and the profits flowed back through European ports from Lisbon and Nantes to Liverpool and Bristol.

From capture to the coast

The crossing did not begin at sea. People were taken in the interior — in wars, raids and kidnappings that fed the trade — then marched to the coast, often in coffles, chained together. On the coast they were held in forts, castles and barracoons while ships were loaded, a wait of weeks or months in which many died before ever boarding.

Great trading castles still stand along the West African coast — such as Cape Coast Castle in Ghana, above — their dungeons a brutal memorial to those held there. Elsewhere on the coast, barracoons (holding compounds) served the same purpose.

Aboard the ships

Below deck, captives were stowed in tiers, packed so tightly that adults could not sit upright. Men were usually kept in the hold, women and children on or nearer the deck; all were kept in irons, the men often shackled in pairs. Ventilation was minimal, disease spread quickly, and dysentery spread through the holds. Sailors brought captives on deck in fair weather to exercise and to feed them — but also, in the words of many accounts, because dead captives were bad business.

The plan above — of the French slaver La Marie-Séraphique of Nantes, fitted for Angola in 1770 — is one of the few surviving contemporary deck plans. It shows, without commentary, how many people were meant to be held in each section of the vessel.

Mortality, and the arithmetic of survival

Between one in six and one in five of those embarked did not reach the Americas; death rates on the worst voyages ran far higher. Death came from disease, dehydration ("the bloody flux"), suffocation in a storm-closed hold, and, in extremis, from despair. These were not accidents: crowding, poor food and withheld care were cost calculations by owners and insurers.

Survival did not end the ordeal. On arrival, survivors were "seasoned" — sold and forced into plantation labour — but the sea crossing was only the sharpest hour of a lifelong regime of enslavement.

Resistance at sea

Captives resisted on the water as they did on land. There were hunger strikes and refusals to eat, plots to seize ships, and open uprisings — dozens are documented, and many surely were not. The most famous is the Amistad in 1839, when Mende captives led by Sengbe Pieh (Cinqué) seized the ship and ultimately won their freedom in a United States court.

Some captives jumped overboard rather than be carried into slavery — an act of resistance, and, by any reckoning, of despair. Survivors' own testimony, such as Olaudah Equiano's narrative of his kidnapping and crossing as a boy, remains among the most direct evidence we have.

Endings and the 'Door of No Return'

Britain abolished its slave trade in 1807 and, with the United States, patrolled the Atlantic to suppress the traffic; the trade continued illegally — especially to Brazil and Cuba — until the 1860s. On Gorée Island, Senegal, the House of Slaves keeps its Door of No Return, through which captives are said to have passed to the waiting ships. The name is a memorial: those who went through it did not come back.

How the numbers are known

Precise totals are impossible — but the record is enormous. The Slave Voyages: Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database compiles details of some 36,000 documented voyages: ships, ports, dates, numbers embarked and landed, and mortality. From these records, scholars estimate roughly 12.5 million people were embarked and about 10.7 million survived the crossing. These are documented figures and modelled extensions of them — a floor, always, rather than a full count, because many voyages left no surviving paper trail.

As with all figures on this site, treat the number as the best current estimate from the evidence, not as a measured certainty.
