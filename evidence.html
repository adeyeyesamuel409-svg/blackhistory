Method

Sources & Evidence

History is only as strong as what it can prove. This page explains how UNBROKEN works — what we treat as evidence, how we handle numbers that nobody truly knows, how we licence the images we show, and how you can check every claim yourself.

Our commitment

Say what we know, and say what we don't

Every factual claim on this site should be traceable to a named source — a primary document, an academic work, or a museum record. Where scholars disagree, we say so. Where a widely repeated figure is a guess dressed up as a fact, we flag it. We would rather be honest about uncertainty than impressive with false precision.

The method

Four rules we hold ourselves to

1

Prefer primary sources

Contemporaneous records — inscriptions, manuscripts, ship logs, eyewitness accounts, archaeology — come first. Later retellings are used only when no primary source survives, and are labelled as such.

2

Name the disagreement

Where historians genuinely differ — population totals, dates, the scale of a trade — we present the range and its source rather than picking one number and calling it fact.

3

Attribute every image

Each plate carries its creator, date and licence. We use public-domain and openly licensed material first, and credit it exactly as its licence requires. Nothing is used to decorate — images are evidence.

4

Make it checkable

The Sources list gives a citation for every factual claim and a link for every image, so a reader can go to the original and judge for themselves.

Reading the numbers

Primary source, estimate, or contested?

Not all evidence is equal, and we mark the difference. The tags below appear throughout the site's deeper pages so you always know what kind of claim you are reading.

Kind of claimWhat it meansExample
Primary A surviving contemporary record or object. The Brookes stowage plan of 1788; the Kouroukan Fouga oral charter of Mali; Aksumite coin inscriptions.
Scholarly consensus Broad agreement among historians, usually built on many sources. That Mansa Musa's 1324 pilgrimage caused a gold price shock in Cairo (reported by al-Umari, widely accepted).
Estimate A modelled or reconstructed figure — useful, but not measured. ~12.5 million people embarked on transatlantic voyages (Slave Voyages database: a documented minimum, not a ceiling).
Contested Scholars actively disagree; the range matters more than any single figure. Totals for the trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean trades, which vary widely between estimates.
Tradition Oral history or heritage memory, valuable but distinct from documentary evidence. The deeds of Nanny of the Maroons, preserved largely through Maroon oral tradition.

We are not infallible. If you find an error or a broken citation, the correction is welcome — accuracy is the whole point.

Images

How we licence what we show

We work down a licence ladder, preferring the most reusable material first:

  • Public domain / CC0 — free of restrictions, used wherever possible.
  • CC BY — credit the creator, exactly as named.
  • CC BY-SA — credit the creator and share alike.

Each image's creator, date, museum or archive, licence and source link is recorded in the on-page caption and in the Sources list.

Why it matters

Evidence, not decoration

A photograph of a surviving object — a brass plaque, a set of shackles, a manuscript page — is itself a piece of history. It can be examined, dated and interpreted.

We avoid staged or speculative imagery where a real source exists. When we must describe something that no image survives of, we say so rather than inventing a picture.
