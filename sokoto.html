The Sokoto Caliphate

Founded by Usman dan Fodio in 1804, the Sokoto Caliphate became the largest state in 19th-century Africa — a federation of emirates bound by scholarship, law and reform.

Origins of the jihād (1804)

Usman dan Fodio (1754–1817), a Fulani scholar trained in the Sokoto region's Islamic academies, challenged the moral and fiscal abuses of the Hausa kings. His 1804 reform movement (jihād) replaced the old aristocracies with emirates ruled under Islamic law, centred on a new capital at Sokoto.

Dan Fodio's movement drew much of its following from the pastoral Fulani, alongside Hausa commoners and scholars who shared his call for reform.

A federation of emirates

The caliphate linked dozens of emirates — Kano, Zaria, Katsina, Adamawa, Gwandu and more — under a single religious and diplomatic authority while leaving local administration to emirs. Its agricultural hinterlands and trans-Saharan trade ties made it an economic powerhouse.

Sokoto, the caliphate's walled capital, photographed and drawn in 1890 — six years before British forces took the city.

Scholarship & law

Usman dan Fodio and his children — Muhammad Bello and Nana Asma'u (1793–1864), a scholar and poet who founded a network of women teachers — produced a large corpus of Arabic and Ajami writing. The caliphate's legal and literary records are among the richest in 19th-century West Africa.

An 1822 manuscript poem by Nana Asma'u, evidence of the caliphate's tradition of women's literacy and learning.

Conquest & legacy

British forces under Frederick Lugard conquered the caliphate in 1903. Its institutions, legal scholarship and identity persisted through the colonial period and continue to shape northern Nigeria today.

The Sultanate survives as a religious and cultural office; dan Fodio's grave and the Sultan's palace in Sokoto remain its enduring symbols.
