Yorubaland

The Oyo Empire

Western Nigeria's dominant state for centuries — a Yoruba empire built on cavalry, constitutional checks and far-reaching trade, from c. 1400 until its 19th-century collapse.

Rise to dominance

Oyo emerged as a Yoruba state around 1400 and, by the 17th century, had become the region's premier power. Its secret cavalry (the Eso), reportedly introduced through Sahelian contact, gave Oyo military superiority on the open savanna where infantry could not follow.

At its height in the late eighteenth century the empire stretched from the Niger bend to the Bight of Benin, its frontier shifting with the fortunes of its client states to the south and west.

Checks on power

The alaafin (king) was deliberately checked by a council of elders and the title of the oyo mesi, whose "itape" could require a king's abdication. This constitutional balance is a striking example of institutional restraint on monarchy in African polities.

The royal court at Oyo-Ile, the capital, was a centre of cast-brass and ironworking; pillars like these once decorated the palace.

Trade & vassals

Oyo taxed and controlled the trade routes to the coast (especially the ports serving Allada and Dahomey), collected tribute from vassal states including Dahomey at its height, and minted influence through arbitration among Yoruba towns.

Oyo-Ile was guarded by a vast system of walls and ditches — among the largest earthworks of pre-colonial West Africa — whose remains still trace the city's outline today.

Collapse & legacy

Civil war and the pressure of the Fulani-led Sokoto expansion weakened Oyo in the early 19th century; Ibadan and other new war states superseded it by the 1830s–1896. Its political ideas (federal checks, crowned kings) influenced later Yoruba states.

The office of alaafin survives to this day; above, the king of Oyo with a British colonial governor c. 1910, and the palace compound as it stood a generation later.
