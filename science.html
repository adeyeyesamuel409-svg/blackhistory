Volume IV · Science, Technology & Knowledge

Knowledge before conquest

Long before the age of European exploration, Africans counted, built, healed, farmed, sailed and wrote. From a notched bone in the Congo to the libraries of Timbuktu, this is a deep history of science, craft and learning.

Reckoning

Counting before writing

The Ishango bone (c. 20,000 years ago) and the Lebombo bone (c. 42,000 years ago) carry deliberate notches. They are widely read as tally or counting tools — among the earliest known anywhere — though whether they encode a calendar or simpler accounting is genuinely debated.

Contested: the Ishango bone's astronomical reading (Marshack) is influential but not settled; simpler tally interpretations remain plausible.

Materials

Iron, steel & furnaces

The Nok culture of Nigeria (c. 1500 BCE–500 CE) produced terracotta and some of West Africa's earliest iron, while Haya smelters in Tanzania used a pre-heated, forced-draft furnace to reach true carbon-steel temperatures roughly 1,500–2,000 years ago.

Contested: whether sub-Saharan ironworking was independently invented or diffused from the north remains a live archaeological question.

Medicine

The body & the cure

Egyptian physicians set fractures, treated wounds and catalogued remedies in the Edwin Smith surgical papyrus and the Ebers papyrus. Trepanation — surgery through the skull — is documented from ancient sites across the continent, and Sahelian scholars later recorded pharmacology and anatomy.

Astronomy

Reading the sky & the year

Cattle pastoralists at Nabta Playa in Egypt, around 5,000 BCE, arranged stones in alignments tracking the solstice — among the world's earliest archaeoastronomy. Egyptian star clocks and, later, Timbuktu's astronomical tables turned the heavens into a calendar.

Engineering

Building at scale

The pyramids and the quarried obelisks of Egypt demanded surveying, ramps and precise stonework. At Great Zimbabwe, masons raised immense walls from cut stone without mortar; at Lalibela, churches were carved downward from living rock; at Djenné, mud was shaped into the world's largest adobe building.

The word

Scripts & scholarship

Africa developed and adapted many writing traditions: Ge'ez in Ethiopia, Tifinagh among the Amazigh, Nsibidi and Ajami in West Africa, Vai in Liberia, and the still-undeciphered Meroitic script of Nubia. Timbuktu alone preserved manuscripts on astronomy, mathematics, medicine and law.
